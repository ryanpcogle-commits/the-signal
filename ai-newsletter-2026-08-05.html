Lead Story

OpenAI's Astra Solves 10 Open Math Problems — for $2,000

An internal version of OpenAI's next frontier model just did real, original mathematics that had stumped human experts for decades. The cost: about two thousand dollars.

On August 1, OpenAI announced that an internal build of Astra — its next major model after the GPT-5 family — solved ten open problems across mathematics and theoretical computer science. The model published formal Lean proofs on GitHub, meaning the results are machine-verifiable, not just plausible-sounding. This is not benchmark-gaming; these were genuine unsolved problems chosen by domain experts.

The implications are hard to overstate. AI has beaten humans at chess, Go, and protein folding. Those victories came in structured, known domains. Original mathematical problem-solving is different: it requires recognizing which tools might apply, forming novel proof strategies, and catching dead ends — the kind of creative reasoning that defines frontier research. If Astra can do this at $2,000 a run, the economics of scientific discovery are about to shift fundamentally.

Separately, OpenAI cut the price of GPT-5.6 Luna by 80% this week — to $0.20 per million input tokens — underscoring that the cost of intelligence is in freefall even as its ceiling continues to rise.

Anthropic Makes Claude Sonnet 5 the Default for Every Free and Pro User

Anthropic shipped Claude Sonnet 5 as its biggest mass-market model release of the year, replacing the previous default for all Free and Pro users worldwide. The new model reportedly performs close to the flagship Opus 4.8 on most tasks — including a 1-million-token context window — while being offered at introductory pricing through the end of August. Policy

1,200 Lab Employees Ask the U.S. Government to Help Slow AI Development

More than 1,200 employees from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and Meta signed an open letter — titled "Pacing the Frontier" — urging the U.S. government to build tools capable of deliberately slowing automated AI development if needed. The signatories include researchers and engineers who work on the most powerful models in existence. The letter is striking not for its policy asks but for its source: the people building the technology are publicly warning that the pace may be outrunning humanity's ability to control it.

Policy

White House Calls OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google to an AI Safety Meeting

The three dominant AI labs convened at the White House on August 3 for a formal AI safety summit. The meeting came amid the broader "Pacing the Frontier" movement and growing congressional interest in AI oversight legislation. No binding commitments were announced, but the meeting signals that the administration is treating AI governance as a near-term priority, not a long-horizon research problem.

Infrastructure

Anaconda Acquires Enkrypt AI — 143,000 MCP Server Vulnerabilities Found

Anaconda announced the acquisition of AI-security startup Enkrypt AI, folding its pre-deployment red-teaming platform — covering 300+ attack categories — into the Anaconda Platform. The deal's headline data point: Enkrypt's scanning found 143,000 vulnerabilities across 73% of MCP servers it tested. As agentic AI infrastructure proliferates, enterprise security tooling is rushing to catch up. Runtime guardrails and NIST/EU AI Act compliance automation now come bundled with the platform.

Policy

Rust Maintainers Ban LLM-Written Code — Sets New Open Source Precedent

The rust-lang/rust project published an official LLM policy on August 5 drawing a sharp line: AI may "answer questions, analyze, distill, refine, check, suggest, review" but not "create." PR authors must disclose any LLM use; LLM-generated code faces stricter testing and scope requirements; non-compliant PRs can be closed without explanation. It is one of the most explicit governance frameworks adopted by a major open-source project and is likely to be cited as a template by other communities navigating the same question.

Business

Google DeepMind Loses John Jumper to Anthropic as Gemini 3.5 Slips

John Jumper — the Nobel Prize-winning researcher behind AlphaFold — has joined Anthropic, the latest in a string of high-profile departures from Google DeepMind. Meanwhile, Google's most powerful model in development, Gemini 3.5 Pro, is reported to be months behind schedule. Axios described the delay as widening the gap with OpenAI and Anthropic. The talent exodus and product delays are fueling questions inside the industry about whether DeepMind can maintain its position as a genuine frontier lab.

5 Patterns Worth Watching

  1. AI is beginning to do original science. Solving open math problems with verifiable proofs is categorically different from benchmark performance. If Astra's results hold, the question of "when does AI contribute to frontier research?" just moved from 2030 to now.
  2. The cost of intelligence is in freefall. GPT-5.6 Luna at $0.20/M tokens — down 80% — means high-volume AI applications that were previously marginal are now economically viable. Price deflation is the accelerant the adoption curve has been waiting for.
  3. The people building AI are asking for the brakes. When 1,200 engineers from the four most powerful AI labs in the world sign a letter asking the government to build a kill switch, that is a signal worth taking seriously — regardless of whether governments act on it.
  4. MCP server security is an unresolved emergency. 143,000 vulnerabilities across 73% of tested servers is not a niche problem. As agents proliferate and MCP becomes foundational infrastructure, unsecured servers become the attack surface for every automated workflow built on top of them.
  5. Google DeepMind's window may be closing. Losing Jumper, slipping on Gemini 3.5, and watching rivals pull ahead on both speed and talent: the lab that co-invented the Transformer is at risk of being lapped by the companies it inspired. The next six months will be defining.
